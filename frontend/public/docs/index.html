How Diacify works, from the top down

A clinician records what they measured at a patient visit. Diacify answers with a diabetes risk assessment. Everything below explains how that sentence turns into a running system, assuming no prior knowledge of this codebase.

The concepts The shape of the system and the five ideas you need before any code makes sense. Read this start to finish, about ten minutes. How it actually works Commands you run against your own machine to watch it accept, refuse, separate and score, then say what happened, out loud.
01

What Diacify is

Diacify is a tool for one kind of person doing one kind of job: a clinician, seeing a patient, who wants to know how that patient's numbers read against a diabetes risk model.

The whole product is three nouns and one verb: a clinician keeps patients, each patient accumulates visits, and recording a visit produces a risk assessment.

No appointments, no analytics dashboard. Both existed in legacy and were deliberately left out ADR-043.

One pass through the product

The clinician doesDiacify recordsThey get back
Signs up, signs in An identity in Supabase Auth A session; everything after this is scoped to them
Adds a patient with a chart reference A patients row: reference, sex A patient in their list, no name, no date of birth
Records a visit: age, blood pressure, BMI, HbA1c, and optionally a lipid panel A visits row, measurements only A risk assessment, scored immediately
Reads the result A risk_assessments row low / medium / high, a 0–100 score, and a confidence flag

A patient is identified by a clinician-chosen reference (a chart label like JD-0412), not by a legal name. Diacify never stores one ADR-038.

02

Two lifecycles, one file

Diacify has two separate lifecycles. One trains a model; the other serves it. Different languages, different workspaces, different times, no shared code, meeting at exactly one file.

Training

Python · run by hand, rarely

  • 662-row CSV dataset
  • parse & clean each field
  • impute missing values
  • engineer ratios & flags
  • assign ADA 2025 labels
  • leakage-safe train/test split
  • fit random forest · 100 trees
  • cross-validate, audit bias
  • export
They meet here backend/src/ml/model.json

Serving

TypeScript · every prediction

  • load & validate model.json at boot
  • visit measurements arrive
  • build 13-value feature vector
  • walk all 100 trees
  • average into 3 probabilities
  • derive score, category, confidence
  • store the assessment

The serving model
A trained model flattened into plain data: no code, no runtime, nothing to execute. Just the numbers a prediction needs.
In Diacify model.json holds 100 decision trees as nested thresholds, the feature order, the class names ["low","medium","high"], and the imputation medians learned during training. It is committed to git alongside the code that reads it, and stamped with a version, currently rf-098c19d0afc9. The Python side writes it; the Node side only ever reads it ADR-025.

What porting the forest into the backend removed

Legacy DiacifyNow
Inference runs in A separate Python service The backend process
A prediction is A network call A function call
If it's unavailable The visit saves unscored Cannot happen
So the schema needed A 'pending' state on every visit Nothing: no row means not scored

Two runtimes became one ADR-001. Training stayed in Python because scikit-learn, cross-validation and bias-audit tooling live there, and it runs on a laptop, not in production.

04

The domain

Four concepts. Two are easy; two carry a distinction that shapes the database, the API, and the UI.

Clinician
The person using Diacify. Owns everything they create, and can never see anything they didn't.
In Diacify A Supabase Auth user. There is no clinicians table of our own: patients.clinician_id references auth.users(id) directly.
Patient
A person under a clinician's care, identified by a label that clinician chose.
In Diacify A patients row holding a reference (1–40 characters, unique per clinician) and sex, which is here for one reason only: it is the single patient-level attribute the trained model actually consumes ADR-014. Everything else clinical belongs to a visit, not the person.
Visit: a fact
An observation. What was measured, on a given date. True regardless of what anyone concludes from it.
In Diacify A visits row: age, systolic, diastolic, BMI and HbA1c are required; RBS, cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL, LDL and VLDL are nullable, because a clinician doesn't draw a full lipid panel every time. There are no score columns on this table at all ADR-018.
Risk assessment: a judgement
A conclusion drawn from a fact by a particular model at a particular time. Can be revisited; can be wrong; can be superseded.
In Diacify A risk_assessments row referencing a visit, stamped with the model_version that produced it. Append-only: one row per (visit, model_version) pair, so retraining adds a second opinion rather than erasing the first ADR-028.
The distinction that shapes the schema

Facts and judgements are stored separately, on purpose. Legacy Diacify put the measurements and the model's output on the same row, so re-scoring a visit overwrote history and a model_version column could only ever mean "the latest one". Splitting them makes "did the retrain move anyone between categories?" an answerable question instead of an erased one.

Score and category answer different questions

Every assessment carries both a risk_score (0–100) and a risk_category (low / medium / high). It is natural to assume one is derived from the other. It isn't, and the reason is the most instructive thing in the domain.

The model outputs three probabilities that sum to 1. The category is simply whichever is largest. The score is a weighted sum (medium counts half, high counts fully), designed for ranking one patient against another. Move the sliders and watch them disagree.

The model's three probabilities

Low is whatever remains; the three always sum to 1, so it isn't independently adjustable.

risk_category: what is this patient?
low

argmax(low, medium, high)

risk_score: how urgently, versus others?
47.50

(0.5 × medium + 1 × high) × 100

low confidence

Why deriving one from the other breaks

Patient low medium high score category
A 0.45 0.15 0.40 47.50 low
B 0.25 0.60 0.15 45.00 medium

A scores higher than B while being categorised lower. Both are correct.

How both can be true at once

A carries a 40% chance of high risk, so A is worth looking at first: the score doing its job. But the model's single best guess for A is still "low", and B's is "medium": the category doing its job.

Band the score into categories and you would report A as the higher-risk patient, contradicting the model. That is why Diacify computes them independently.

Low confidence
A signal that the model's top answer wasn't a clear winner, so a clinician shouldn't read the category as settled.
In Diacify Set when the highest of the three probabilities falls below 0.55. The threshold has to sit above 1/3 to mean anything at all: with three classes the top probability can never be lower than that, so an earlier 0.40 threshold was advertised but literally unreachable.
05

The one idea to hold onto

If you remember one thing

The backend does not decide who owns what data. It forwards the clinician's own token to Postgres and lets the database answer. No route handler in Diacify contains a clinician_id filter, not one, and that is not an oversight.

Row-level security (RLS)
A rule attached to a table itself, saying which rows the current user is allowed to touch. The database enforces it on every query, no matter who is asking or what they asked for.
In Diacify Each request builds a Supabase client from the caller's own JWT, so queries run as that clinician ADR-013. A SELECT * FROM patients with no WHERE clause returns only their patients, because Postgres silently adds the condition.

Ownership gets further from the row at each level, so each table proves it by walking back up the chain.

patients Owned directly
clinician_id = auth.uid()

The row names its owner, so the check is one comparison.

visits Owned through one join
exists ( select 1 from patients p where p.id = visits.patient_id and p.clinician_id = auth.uid() )

Nothing on a visit names a clinician, so ownership is proved by looking at its patient ADR-017.

risk_assessments Owned through two joins
exists ( select 1 from visits v join patients p on p.id = v.patient_id where v.id = risk_assessments.visit_id and p.clinician_id = auth.uid() )

Assessment → visit → patient → clinician, resolved in one expression.

Why not just put a clinician_id on every table?

It reads more simply, and it is a common design. It also leaves the write path open. Follow one insert down both routes:

Why the column isn't worth keeping alongside the join

Closing that write hole requires the join check anyway. Once it is there, the denormalized column adds nothing to enforcement: it just becomes a second place ownership is recorded, and therefore a second place it can disagree with itself.

Legacy Diacify checked this precedent: it scoped visits through patients too, and denormalized only onto appointments, a different table with a different reason ADR-017.

What follows from this one decision

  • No route handler mentions a clinician. There is nothing to forget to filter by.
  • Another clinician's patient returns 404, not 403: invisible rather than forbidden, so the API never confirms someone else's record exists.
  • A bad patient_id surfaces as a Postgres 42501 rather than a clean validation error ADR-008.

Set up, once

Concepts JWT (JSON Web Token) Access token Bearer auth Local dev stack

Everything below this point is something you run. Three terminals, then a token you keep for the rest of the page. Every command here was run against this project before it was written down.

Terminal 1Start the database and auth service

bash
supabase start

Terminal 2Start the API

bash
npm run dev --workspace backend

Terminal 3Become a clinician, and keep the token

bash
export ANON_KEY=$(supabase status -o env | grep '^ANON_KEY' | cut -d= -f2- | tr -d '"')

# Creates the practice clinician the first time; harmlessly does nothing after that.
curl -s http://127.0.0.1:54321/auth/v1/signup \
  -H "apikey: $ANON_KEY" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"email":"practice-a@example.com","password":"practice-password-123"}' > /dev/null

# Signing in is what actually gets the token, so this works every time you come back.
export TOKEN=$(curl -s "http://127.0.0.1:54321/auth/v1/token?grant_type=password" \
  -H "apikey: $ANON_KEY" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"email":"practice-a@example.com","password":"practice-password-123"}' \
  | python3 -c "import sys,json; print(json.load(sys.stdin)['access_token'])")

echo "${TOKEN:0:24}..."
What just happened

You signed up as a clinician and got back a JWT, the same thing the real frontend holds after someone signs in. Every command from here on sends it, and everything the API does is decided by what is inside it.

Say it like this "Signing in gets you a signed token. The backend never stores a session; the token itself carries who you are, and the backend checks the signature on every request."

The API surface

Concepts HTTP methods Safe vs. idempotent REST resource nesting HTTP status semantics Pagination Query-param filtering CORS Idempotency

The verbs, before the endpoints

Two words describe what a method promises. Both are about the effect on the server, not about the status code that comes back.

Safe
The call only reads. Nothing on the server changes.
Idempotent
Call it once or call it five times: the server ends up in the same state either way.

Four methods, one card each: what it does in a line, what you send, and what comes back.

GET /api/patients/:id Safe

Reads one patient. It changes nothing.

You send

bash
curl -s http://localhost:3000/api/patients/$PID \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN"

You get back

200 OK{
  "data": {
    "id": "3f9a1c7e-...",
    "clinician_id": "b21d4e60-...",
    "created_at": "2026-09-11T09:14:22.418Z",
    "sex": "male",
    "reference": "PRAC-001"
  }
}

Read it 100 times and the 100th call returns the same row as the first.

POST /api/patients/:id/visits Not safe · Not idempotent

Records a new visit, and scores it in the same call.

You send

bash
curl -s -X POST http://localhost:3000/api/patients/$PID/visits \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"visit_date":"2026-08-15","age":50,"systolic":120,
       "diastolic":80,"bmi":25,"hba1c":5.5}'

You get back

201 CREATED{
  "data": {
    "id": "c48b0d12-...",
    "patient_id": "3f9a1c7e-...",
    "visit_date": "2026-08-15",
    "age": 50,
    "risk_assessment": {
      "model_version": "...",
      "risk_score": 8,
      "risk_category": "low",
      "low_confidence": false,
      "created_at": "2026-09-11T09:15:03.902Z"
    }
  }
}

Send that same body twice and you get two visits, not one. A client that retries after a dropped response double-records a real visit.

PATCH /api/patients/:id Idempotent

Edits a patient who already exists.

You send

bash
curl -s -X PATCH http://localhost:3000/api/patients/$PID \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"reference":"PRAC-001","sex":"female"}'

You get back

200 OK{
  "data": {
    "id": "3f9a1c7e-...",
    "clinician_id": "b21d4e60-...",
    "created_at": "2026-09-11T09:14:22.418Z",
    "sex": "female",
    "reference": "PRAC-001"
  }
}

Send that body twice and the patient ends up the same. But you have to send every field, not only the one you are changing. The box below explains why that is a gap.

DELETE /api/patients/:id Idempotent

Removes a patient, and every visit and assessment under them.

You send

bash
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code}\n" \
  -X DELETE http://localhost:3000/api/patients/$PID \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN"

You get back

204 NO CONTENT(empty body)

The first call returns 204 and the second returns 404. Different codes, same end state: no such patient. Idempotent is about the effect, not about the response.

Not PUT, because PUT can create a resource at an id that does not exist yet, and this endpoint returns 404 instead. The name is only half earned though: the body reuses the create schema, so both fields are required and a one-field edit is rejected with 400.

Drill AAsk for two patients. What comes back besides the two?

bash
curl -s "http://localhost:3000/api/patients?limit=2" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" | python3 -m json.tool | head -4
What you should see

{ data, page, limit, total }: two rows, but total counts every patient matching the query, not the page.

Say it like this "List endpoints return the page plus the total count, so the client can render pagination without a second request to ask how many there are."

Drill BAsk for 9999 patients, then ask for "abc" of them. Should either be an error?

bash
curl -s "http://localhost:3000/api/patients?limit=9999" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" | python3 -c "import sys,json; print('limit:', json.load(sys.stdin)['limit'])"

curl -s -o /dev/null -w "limit=abc -> %{http_code}\n" \
  "http://localhost:3000/api/patients?limit=abc" -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN"
What you should see: they are treated differently on purpose

9999 comes back as 100 clamped, but abc is 400.

"Give me everything" is a reasonable thing to want, so it is quietly capped rather than refused. "Give me abc" is not a request anyone meant to make; it is a bug in the caller, and silently defaulting it would hide that bug.

Say it like this "An out-of-range value gets clamped because the intent is clear; a malformed one gets rejected, because guessing what the caller meant would mask a client bug."

Drill CFilter the list server-side. Does total follow the filter, or ignore it?

bash
for q in "risk=high" "risk=unscored" "sort=risk"; do
  echo -n "?$q -> "
  curl -s "http://localhost:3000/api/patients?$q" -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" \
    | python3 -c "import sys,json; print('total', json.load(sys.stdin)['total'])"
done
What you should see

Each filter reports its own total. Filtering happens in the database, not in the browser.

That distinction is the whole point: filtering a fetched page in JavaScript would only ever search the twenty rows already downloaded, so a high-risk patient sitting at position 400 would never appear.

Say it like this "Filtering and sorting are done in the database so they apply to the whole table. Doing it client-side only filters the page you already fetched, which quietly gives wrong answers once the data outgrows one page."

Drill DSend a PATCH with only one field. Does it apply just that field, or refuse the whole request?

bash
curl -s -w "\n%{http_code}\n" -X PATCH http://localhost:3000/api/patients/$PID \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"sex":"female"}'
What you should see

400 Invalid patient data

Refused outright, not partially applied. A caller has to resend reference too, even to change nothing about it, which is the PUT behaviour the article you're checking this against describes, wearing a PATCH label.

Say it like this "We use PATCH instead of PUT mainly because PUT implies the endpoint can create a resource at that id, and this one can't: a missing patient 404s rather than being created. But the request body itself isn't genuinely partial yet; it reuses the create schema, so you have to resend both fields. That's a real gap between the verb and the behaviour, not a considered trade-off."

Drill ETwo POST endpoints exist for the same visit: creating it, and re-scoring it. Do they agree on idempotency?

bash
# create the same visit twice
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "create #1 -> %{http_code}\n" http://localhost:3000/api/patients/$PID/visits \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"visit_date":"2026-08-15","age":50,"systolic":120,"diastolic":80,"bmi":25,"hba1c":5.5}'
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "create #2 -> %{http_code}\n" http://localhost:3000/api/patients/$PID/visits \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"visit_date":"2026-08-15","age":50,"systolic":120,"diastolic":80,"bmi":25,"hba1c":5.5}'
curl -s "http://localhost:3000/api/patients/$PID/visits" -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" \
  | python3 -c "import sys,json; print('total visits:', json.load(sys.stdin)['total'])"
What you should see

201 twice, and total visits: 2. Not one, two.

Compare that against Drill F, immediately below: scoring the same visit twice with POST /visits/:id/predict produced exactly one row, not two. Both endpoints use the same HTTP method. One is idempotent, one isn't, because idempotency isn't a property of the verb, it's a property of what the handler does. Scoring was deliberately made idempotent with a database constraint; creating a visit never was, because two visits on the same day are a real, legitimate thing a clinician might do.

Say it like this "POST isn't idempotent by default; it's idempotent only where we've deliberately made it so, like the scoring endpoint's unique constraint. Recording a visit stays non-idempotent on purpose, because a clinician genuinely might record two real visits with the same measurements."

Drill FScore the same visit twice. Two assessments, or one?

bash
export VID=$(curl -s "http://localhost:3000/api/patients/$PID/visits" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" \
  | python3 -c "import sys,json; print(json.load(sys.stdin)['data'][0]['id'])")

curl -s -X POST "http://localhost:3000/api/visits/$VID/predict" -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" > /dev/null
curl -s -X POST "http://localhost:3000/api/visits/$VID/predict" -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" > /dev/null

docker exec supabase_db_diacify psql -U postgres -tAX \
  -c "select count(*) from risk_assessments where visit_id='$VID';"
What you should see

1

Scoring is deterministic: the same visit under the same model always gives the same answer, so a repeat is a duplicate, not new information. A unique constraint on (visit, model version) makes the second write collide, and the existing row is read back and returned instead.

Say it like this "Re-scoring is idempotent. The database has a unique constraint on the visit and model version, so a retry collides and we return the row that's already there rather than writing a second one."

Drill GThe CORS one. Request the API pretending to be a malicious website. Does it get the data?

bash
# the browser's preflight question
curl -s -D - -o /dev/null -X OPTIONS http://localhost:3000/api/patients \
  -H "Origin: https://evil.example.com" | grep -iE "^HTTP|access-control-allow-origin"

# now an actual request claiming that origin
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "real request -> %{http_code}\n" http://localhost:3000/api/patients \
  -H "Origin: https://evil.example.com" -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN"
What you should see, and the misconception it kills

The header comes back as Access-Control-Allow-Origin: http://localhost:5173 (the one allowed origin, not the evil one). But the real request still returns 200 with the data.

CORS did not block anything. The server answered normally; it simply declined to grant permission. A browser would refuse to hand that response to the malicious page's JavaScript. curl has no such rule, so it reads the body happily.

This is why CORS is not access control. The thing actually protecting that data is the bearer token, which the evil page does not have.

Say it like this "CORS is enforced by the browser, not the server; it stops a malicious page reading a response using someone's logged-in session. It's not authentication, and it does nothing against a direct server-side request. We pin it to one origin rather than using a wildcard because every request carries a bearer token."

08

Watch a request get stopped

Concepts Middleware JWT signature verification Public-key crypto (JWKS) Rolling-window rate limiting Schema validation HTTP status codes

A write passes four checks before anything is saved. Each one refuses with a different status code, and each refusal is something you can trigger on purpose.

Drill 1Send no token at all. What comes back?

bash
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code}\n" http://localhost:3000/api/patients
What you should see

401

The request never reaches any patient code. The first gate looks for an Authorization header, finds nothing, and answers immediately.

Say it like this "Authentication is middleware; it runs before any route handler, so an unauthenticated request never touches the database at all."

Drill 2Send a token that is real text but not a real token.

bash
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code}\n" http://localhost:3000/api/patients \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer not-a-real-token"
What you should see, and the bit that matters

401

This is the answer to "how does the backend actually validate the token?", and it does it without calling the auth service. A token is signed with a private key. The backend holds the matching public key (fetched once and cached), and checks the signature by maths, locally.

That is why a forged token fails instantly: you cannot produce a valid signature without the private key, which only the auth service has.

Say it like this "The backend verifies the token's signature locally against the auth provider's public key. There's no lookup and no network call on the hot path: a tampered token fails the signature check, and an expired one fails on the expiry claim inside it."

Drill 3Now send your real token.

bash
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code}\n" http://localhost:3000/api/patients \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN"
What you should see

200

Same URL, same server, three different outcomes decided entirely by one header.

Drill 4Create a patient, then send a visit with an impossible HbA1c of 99.

bash
export PID=$(curl -s http://localhost:3000/api/patients \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"reference":"PRAC-001","sex":"male"}' \
  | python3 -c "import sys,json; print(json.load(sys.stdin)['data']['id'])")

curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code}\n" http://localhost:3000/api/patients/$PID/visits \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"age":58,"systolic":148,"diastolic":94,"bmi":33.2,"hba1c":99}'
What you should see

400

HbA1c is a percentage; 99 is not a possible human reading. The schema caps it at 20, so this never reaches the database. Validation happens once, at the edge; after that, the rest of the code trusts the values.

Say it like this "Untrusted input is parsed once at the boundary into a typed shape. Nothing downstream re-checks it, because nothing downstream can receive anything that didn't pass."

Drill 5Try to smuggle in a field the API never asked for.

bash
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code}\n" http://localhost:3000/api/patients/$PID/visits \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"age":58,"systolic":148,"diastolic":94,"bmi":33.2,"hba1c":7.1,
       "patient_id":"someone-elses-patient-id"}'
What you should see

400

Rejected, not ignored. The schema is strict, so an unexpected field is an error rather than something quietly dropped. That matters here specifically: which patient a visit belongs to comes from the URL and the database's own ownership rule, never from the body.

Say it like this "The schema rejects unknown fields rather than stripping them, so an attacker gets told no instead of guessing which fields silently do something."

Say it like this "Rate limiting is keyed on the authenticated user id, which is exactly why it has to run after authentication: the limiter literally can't count until it knows whose requests it's counting."

10

The database underneath

Concepts Schema design UUID primary keys Foreign keys & cascade Composite indexes Unique & CHECK constraints Numeric precision Migrations

Three tables. Every column type and every index is a decision, and most of them are defending against something specific.

Choices worth being able to defend

ChoiceWhy not the alternative
uuid primary keys An auto-incrementing integer leaks how many patients exist and makes ids guessable. With ids appearing in URLs, that invites probing for other clinicians' records
numeric(4,2) for HbA1c A float can't store 5.7 exactly. For a clinical measurement that gets compared against a diagnostic threshold, that rounding is not acceptable
double precision for probabilities The opposite case: these must reproduce scikit-learn's float64 output exactly, and a fixed-scale numeric would round it away
One index on (patient_id, visit_date) A separate patient_id index would be redundant: a composite index already serves lookups on its leading column, so the extra one costs write speed for nothing
Nullable lipid columns A clinician doesn't draw a full panel every visit. NOT NULL would force fake values into clinical records

Drill HLook at what indexes actually exist. Predict how many before you run it.

bash
docker exec supabase_db_diacify psql -U postgres -tAX -c \
  "select tablename, indexname from pg_indexes
   where schemaname='public'
     and tablename in ('patients','visits','risk_assessments')
   order by tablename;"
What you should see

Seven, and only three were written by hand:

  • idx_patients_clinician_id: every query filters by owner, so this is the one that matters most
  • idx_patients_clinician_id_reference: unique, which is what makes a reference unique per clinician rather than globally
  • idx_visits_patient_date: composite, serving both "this patient's visits" and "newest first" from one index

The other four are the three primary keys plus the unique constraint on risk_assessments, which Postgres backs with an index automatically.

Say it like this "Indexes follow the access patterns: everything is read filtered by owner, and visits are read newest-first per patient, so there's a composite index covering both. We deliberately didn't add a standalone patient_id index because the composite's leading column already serves it."

Drill IReuse a patient reference you already used. Which code: 400, 409, or 500?

bash
curl -s -w "\n%{http_code}\n" http://localhost:3000/api/patients \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"reference":"PRAC-001","sex":"male"}'
What you should see

409 Reference already in use

The database raises a unique-violation; the route recognises that specific error and turns it into 409. Without that mapping it would surface as a generic 500, technically "an error happened", but useless to the person who just needs to pick a different label.

Note the constraint is on (clinician_id, reference), not on reference alone: another clinician can use PRAC-001 quite happily.

Say it like this "Uniqueness is enforced by a composite database constraint, so it holds even under concurrent requests: checking first and then inserting would leave a race between the two. The route maps the constraint violation to a 409 so the client gets a useful answer instead of a 500."

Drill JDelete a patient who has a visit and an assessment. What happens to them?

bash
export CID=$(curl -s http://localhost:3000/api/patients \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"reference":"CASCADE-DEMO","sex":"female"}' \
  | python3 -c "import sys,json; print(json.load(sys.stdin)['data']['id'])")

curl -s -o /dev/null http://localhost:3000/api/patients/$CID/visits \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"age":58,"systolic":148,"diastolic":94,"bmi":33.2,"hba1c":7.1}'

COUNT="select (select count(*) from visits where patient_id='$CID') as visits,
       (select count(*) from risk_assessments a join visits v on v.id=a.visit_id
        where v.patient_id='$CID') as assessments;"

docker exec supabase_db_diacify psql -U postgres -tAX -c "$COUNT"
curl -s -o /dev/null -X DELETE http://localhost:3000/api/patients/$CID -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN"
docker exec supabase_db_diacify psql -U postgres -tAX -c "$COUNT"
What you should see

1|1 before the delete, 0|0 after, and the API returned 204, no body.

One DELETE against one table removed rows from three, because each foreign key declares on delete cascade. The application never wrote any cleanup code, and so it cannot forget to.

Say it like this "Cleanup is declared in the schema with cascading foreign keys, not written in application code, so there's no path that deletes a patient and leaves orphaned visits behind."

Drill KSend a 41-character reference. The database allows 40. Where does it get caught?

bash
curl -s -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code}\n" http://localhost:3000/api/patients \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"reference":"AAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAA","sex":"male"}'
What you should see

400, not 500.

Two layers agree on the same rule. The schema rejects it at the edge, so the request never reaches Postgres, but the table also carries a CHECK (char_length(reference) between 1 and 40) constraint as a backstop, for anything that ever reaches the database by another route.

Say it like this "Validation lives at the edge so the caller gets a clean 400, and the same rule is a database constraint underneath, so the data can't become invalid even if something bypasses the API."

Migrations are forward-only, one concern each, committed to git. There is no "edit the schema in a dashboard" step: the thirteen files in supabase/migrations/ are the schema's whole history, applied in filename order. CI replays all of them against an empty database before any test runs, so a migration that only works on your machine fails the pull request rather than production.

12

How the model learned

Concepts Train/test split Data leakage Stratified sampling Cross-validation Hyperparameter tuning Bias audit

This part runs on a laptop, not in production. It reads a CSV of 662 real patients and writes the model file the API loads at startup.

Why split first? If you filled in missing values using the whole dataset, the averages would be partly computed from the test patients. The model would then be tested on data it had already peeked at, and its accuracy would look better than it really is.

The labels are a clinical rule, not a guess

Nobody hand-labelled 662 patients. A rule assigns each one, in two stages:

Stage 1 Start from HbA1c

Under 5.7% is low, 5.7–6.4% is medium, 6.5% and above is high: the diabetes diagnostic thresholds from the ADA 2025 Standards of Care.

Stage 2 Upgrade if other things are wrong

Count five warning signs: high blood pressure, obesity, high blood sugar, and two bad cholesterol ratios. Two or more, and the patient moves up one level. It never moves anyone down.

Four of those five warning signs are also fed to the model as inputs. The rule decides what a training patient is called; the model learns to predict that call from the numbers.

14

What you'd improve

Concepts Horizontal scaling Observability Test coverage gaps Shared state

Being able to name the weak spots is worth more than pretending there aren't any. Four real ones, and what each would take.

  • Frontend test coverage. Four tests. The forms and the risk display have no safety net: a rendering regression ships silently.
  • The rate limiter only works on one server. It counts in memory, so running two copies of the backend would give every clinician double the budget. Fixing it means moving the count into the database or a shared cache.
  • Errors are logged but not searchable. A failure writes a line to the console. There is no way to ask "how often did this happen last week" without structured logging.
  • Database queries are written inline in the route handlers, except for one. It's fine while each query is used once, but it means there's no single place to look when you want to know how a table is read.

Say it like this "The biggest gap is frontend test coverage. The backend is well covered because that's where the ownership and scoring logic is, but a UI regression wouldn't be caught. After that, the rate limiter is per-process, so it only holds correctly on a single instance."

15

Check yourself

Answer these out loud, in full sentences, before opening them. Saying it is the skill being practised; recognising the right answer when you read it is not the same thing.

Q1Someone asks "is Diacify a Python app or a Node app?" Why is that question malformed?

Because it conflates the two lifecycles. Training is Python and runs offline on a laptop, rarely, by hand. Serving is TypeScript and runs in the backend on every prediction. The running application in production contains no Python at all: it reads model.json, which Python produced earlier. The correct answer is "Node in production; Python produced the model it serves."

Q2Why doesn't the visits table have a risk_score column?

A visit is a fact: what was measured. A score is a judgement: what one model concluded about those measurements. Storing them on one row means re-scoring overwrites the previous conclusion, which destroys the ability to compare model versions and makes the stored model_version meaningless. They live in separate tables, and assessments are append-only per (visit, model_version).

Q3Patient A's score is 47.5 and category is low; patient B's score is 45.0 and category is medium. Is that a bug?

No, they answer different questions. The category is the model's single most likely class (argmax). The score is a ranking number weighting medium at half and high at full. A is a genuinely split case carrying 40% probability of high risk, so it ranks above B for attention, while its single best guess is still "low". Deriving the category by banding the score is the actual bug: it would report A as higher-risk than B, contradicting the model.

Q4You're reviewing a new endpoint. It filters by clinician_id in the query. What do you say?

That it shouldn't need to. Queries run through a Supabase client built from the caller's own JWT, so RLS already restricts every statement to that clinician's rows. A hand-written filter is at best redundant, and at worst misleading: it implies the application is the thing enforcing ownership, which invites someone later to write an endpoint that forgets. The database is the enforcement point; the application never gets a vote.

Q5Why does requesting another clinician's patient by id return 404 rather than 403?

Because RLS doesn't hide the row from the response; it hides it from the query. The backend genuinely finds nothing, so "not found" is the honest answer. It's also the safer one: a 403 would confirm that a record with that id exists and belongs to someone else, which is information the caller shouldn't have.

Q6A clinician reports rate-limit errors on visits they never sent. Where do you look first?

Whether they're being routed to more than one backend instance. The rate limiter keys its count on req.user.id in an in-memory Map, per process: two instances keep two separate counts, which doesn't explain extra rejections on its own, but does mean the limiter's real budget silently depends on how many instances are running. The fix, if it were needed, is a shared store (Postgres or Redis), not a higher limit.

Q7What does Math.fround in forest.ts actually protect against?

scikit-learn compares split thresholds in 32-bit float precision; a plain JavaScript number is 64-bit. A feature value can sit in the gap between a threshold and the nearest float32 above it, close enough to be "the same number" at float64 precision, but on the other side of the split once cast to float32. Without Math.fround first, that value walks a different path through the tree and can land in a different leaf, occasionally changing the predicted category. Confirmed with a real near-threshold case in forest.test.ts, not just reasoned about.

Q8service_role usually bypasses RLS entirely. Why does Diacify hold it to zero table grants?

Because nothing in the running application currently needs to act across every clinician's rows at once: every real operation is scoped to one authenticated clinician already. A credential that can ignore every ownership check is a standing attack surface (a leaked key, a compromised script) proportional to what it can reach, not to what actually uses it. Holding it at zero costs nothing today and removes that surface entirely; it can be widened deliberately, migration by migration, the day something genuinely needs it.
